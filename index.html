      How it decides what you can see

      Every 10 seconds the page gets positions from public ADS-B receivers. Each second it moves every plane forward along its heading. A plane counts as one you can see when:

      • It's at least 10° above the horizon, clear of most trees and roofs.
      • By day, it looks at least 5 arcminutes wide, about a sixth of the Moon. That's a typical wingspan for its size class over its distance from you.
      • At night, its lights are within 25 miles, or 10 miles for small planes and helicopters.
      • It's more than 15° from the sun.
      • Its position is less than a minute old.

      The Moon counts when it's 10° up, and by day only when it's more than a sliver. Venus counts from sunset, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn once the sky is dark. The ISS and China's Tiangong station count when your sky is dark but sunlight still reaches them, which is usually in the hours after sunset or before sunrise.

      These cutoffs are estimates. Haze, clouds, contrails and your eyes all change the answer.

      What this data can't see

      • Planes without ADS-B. The FAA requires it in busy airspace and above 10,000 feet, not everywhere.
      • Low planes behind hills. Receivers need a clear line of sight.
      • Where a plane is going. Planes don't broadcast routes, and this page doesn't guess.
      • Some identities. FAA privacy addresses hide the registration and type.
      • Perfect angles. Phone compasses drift, so line up on the sun.
